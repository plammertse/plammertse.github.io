15   Viscous loss in the rotationally optimal propeller

15.1   Viscous loss in the rotationally optimized propeller

The local momentum loss ratio for the optimal rotational distribution was given before by (11.8). It is constant along the blade, but pro­port­ional to the load : both to the local load and to the overall load.

  By contrast, the viscous loss ratio (14.14) does vary with the radius. But unlike the momentum loss, it is in­de­pen­dent of the load. It is always the same, regardless of the load distribution, whether rotationally optimal or not.

  Adding (11.8) and (14.14), the combined local loss ratio for the rotationally optimal propeller is :

d  P ^ m d  T ^   +   d  P ^ v d  T ^ = ½ . T ^ κ φ   +   ε . x cos 2 φ (15.1)

Figure 16.1 shows the graph. It is the same as figure 14.4 for the viscous loss alone, only shifted up by the optimal, constant momentum loss. Clearly, the combined local loss ratio is not constant along the blade. Neither is the marginal loss, as we shall see later. The rotationally optimized propeller is not globally optimal in the pres­ence of viscous loss.

  We will do the optimization in the next chapter. For now, we continue with the rotationally optimal propeller.

Figure 15.1 :   Local combined loss ratio (15.1), rotationally optimal thrust.

15.2   The viscous loss integral for light loading

We wish to find the overall loss for the rotationally optimal propeller, including the viscous loss. We found the integral of the momentum loss before, so we will now look at the viscous loss integral.

  For lightly loaded propellers without tip loss, the local thrust d T ^ follows from (7.14), repeated here with the explicit argument on b (x) added in for clarity :

d T ^   =   b ( x ) . 2  x . d x X 2 (15.2)

With the viscous loss ratio of (14.14) for an arbitrary loading b (x), the local viscous power loss will be :

d P ^ v   =   ε . x cos 2 φ . b ( x ) . 2  x . d x X 2 (15.3)

The light loading viscous loss integral, before tip loss, for an arbitrary induction distri­bution b (x) is then :

P ^ v   =   ε . ∫ 0 X b  ( x ) cos 2 φ . 2  x 2 . d x X 2 (15.4)

15.3   The rotationally optimal propeller

In the rotationally optimal propeller, the induction distribution b (x) follows from (11.11), repeated here :

b ( x ) = T ^  .  cos 2 φ κ φ (15.5)

When substituting this into (15.3), the cos 2 φ terms cancel :

P ^ v = ε . T ^ κ φ . 1 X 2  . ∫ 0 X 2  x 2 . d  x (15.6)

The integral by itself yields ⅔ X 3. Dividing out an X 2 and the T ^ term, the overall viscous loss for the rotationally optimal propeller is :

P ^ v T ^ = ε . ⅔  X κ φ (15.7)

This result makes good sense. The numerator ⅔ X is the average value for the x term in the viscous loss, and from (11.3) the κφ term in the denominator is the known average value of cos2 φ over the prop disk.

  Comparing (15.6) to (14.14) confirms the classical rule of thumb that the overall viscous efficiency of a pro­peller can be approximated by the local viscous efficiency at the "average" radius of 0.7 R.

  Figure 15.2 shows (15.6) against the tip speed ratio X, using a blade section drag of ε = 0.02 as an example. The shape is similar to the plot of the local viscous loss ratio against x in figure 14.4, but this time the minimum occurs near X = 1.35 instead of at x = 1, and the asymptote for large values of X  is ⅔ ε . X  instead of ε . x.

Figure 15.2 :   The total viscous loss ratio in the rotationally optimal propeller,
            as a function of the tip speed ratio X.

  Loss ratios and efficiencies cannot be integrated over the propeller disk. They will have to be weighted by the local thrust. We will have to integrate the local losses themselves, not the local loss ratios. As a counter-example, it will not help if half the disk area has a perfect zero percent loss ratio, if all the thrust is on the non-perfect half.

15.4   The overall loss for the rotationally optimal distribution

We can now sum the overall ( averaged ) values of the momentum and the viscous losses (10.10) and (15.6). Note that these are values for the rotationally optimal propeller, for light loading, before tip loss, and before viscous optimization :

P ^ m+v T ^   ≡   P ^ m T ^   +   P ^ v T ^ = ½ . T ^ κ φ   +   ⅔  ε  .  X κ φ (15.8)

  Figure 15.3 shows the result. The basis is a constant loss due to the optimized momentum loss, and a straight slope due to the non-optimized viscous loss. The viscous loss term gives a steady rise of the combined loss ratio with increasing tip speed ratio X.

  The factor 1 ⁄ κφ (X) which restores the design thrust after the rotational optimization, adds a sharply curved sweep-up for the lower X values to the straight slope.

Figure 15.3 :   Overall combined loss ratio (15.8), for rotationally optimal thrust.

15.4   The optimal tip speed ratio

The loss increase at both ends of the X range means that there is a minimum halfway. The location of the optimum depends on the balance T ^ ⁄  ε between the momentum and viscous losses.

  This balance will not change if we scale up both T ^ and ε by the same factor. The whole graph will just be inflated by that factor. But if we change either T ^ or ε separately, the curve will change. The figure shows the effect of varying ε for a given T ^ . The circle marker indi­cat­es this minimum for our standard example of T ^  = 0.1, ε = 0.02. The optimum falls near X = 2.9.

  For large ε, the optimum shifts to the purely viscous optimum of figure 15.2, at X = 1.35. For very low ε, the optimum shifts to very high X, where the momentum loss gets ever smaller.

  These are optimum tip speed ratios before tip loss, and so they apply only in a duct. Later chapters will add the tip loss. This will shift the optimum for the tip speed ratio X to higher values.
