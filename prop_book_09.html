9   Rotational momentum loss

9.1   Local loss and local efficiency

Compared to the axial actuator disk, the pitch angle of the blade elements causes an extra induction u, at right angles to the axial induction v. This leaves extra kinetic energy in the wake which shows up as an extra momen­tum loss. Using the far wake values w and w ′ in figure 7.2, we have :

w  ′ = w cos φ (9.1)

With the definition w ≡ b . V  from (4.1), the kinetic energy dPm left behind in the far wake of a ring becomes :

d P m = ½  d m • . ( w  ′ ) 2 = ½  d m • .  V 2 . b 2 cos 2 φ (9.2)

Dividing by the local thrust (7.5) gives us the local momentum loss ratio :

d P m d T . V = ½  . b 2 cos 2 φ b . d m • . V 2 d m • . V 2 = ½  .  b ( x ) cos 2 φ (9.3)

This local efficiency depends only on the local induction ratio. It does not depend on the local mass flow !

  Compared to the axial loss ratio of (4.16), the rotation increases the momentum loss by a factor of 1 ⁄ cos2 φ.

  We can reach the same result by the non-dimensional route. Applying the steps from (7.15) tot (7.17) to the ring momentum loss (7.2) gives :

d P m  ′ = ½  b 2 cos 2 φ . 2  x . d x X 2  .  ( 1 + ½  b ) (9.4)

Dividing by the ring thrust (7.14), the 2x . dx ⁄ X 2 terms cancel, leaving only a 1 ⁄ cos2 φ term and the terms in b :

d P m  ′ d T  ′ = ½  b 2 cos 2 φ . 1 b . ( 1 + ½  b ) ( 1 + ½  b ) (9.5)

Like in the dimensional (9.3), one term of b and both the heavy loading terms in dm ′ cancel, leaving only :

d P m  ′ d T  ′ = ½  b cos 2 φ (9.6)

Once again, this ratio is independent of the local mass flow. By (3.9) and (3.10), the ring efficiency becomes :

d η m = 1 1 + d P m   ′ d T   ′ = 1 1 + ½  . b ( x ) cos 2 φ (9.7)

The symbol dηm, with a "d" in front, is used here loosely for the local momentum efficiency, but is not an infini­tesimal. Local ratios and efficiencies cannot be integrated over the rings like the overall thrust and power can.

9.2   Integrating the rotational loss

For the overall efficiency, we cannot integrate the power loss ratio (9.4) over the propeller. As a counterexample, this will not work if one half of the propeller has a power loss ratio of maybe zero, but the only thrust is on the other half.

  We will have to separately integrate the power and the thrust over the propeller, and find the ratio of those two overall values. We do the integration in non-dimensional form.

  We can make (9.3) a non-dimensional momentum power loss by the definition of (3.3) :

dP  ′ = ½ . d m • . V 2 ρ V 3 . π R 2 . b2 cos 2 φ (9.8)

Following the steps from (7.9) to (7.11), for lightly loaded propellers we have :

dm  ′ = d m • . V 2 ρ V 3 . π R 2 ≅ 2 x . d x X 2 (9.9)

This turns (9.6) into :

d P m  ′ = ½  . b 2 ( x ) cos 2 φ . 2  x . d x X 2 (9.10)

The integral is :

P m  ′ = 1 X 2 . ∫ 0 X ½ . b2 cos 2 φ . 2 x . dx (9.11)

This is the generic, non-dimensional, overall momentum loss for a lightly loaded propeller including rotation, at an arbitrary induction ratio distribution b (x). The matching thrust integral was given by (7.17). The ratio of the two integrals yields the overall loss ratio (3.9) :

P m  ′ T  ′ = ∫ d P m  ′ ∫ d T  ′ = ∫ 0 X ½ . b2 cos 2 φ . 2 x . dx ∫ 0 X b . 2 x . dx (9.12)

This equation may look a bit daunting at first, but we will derive an optimum distribution b(x) which not only mini­mizes the rotational loss, but also gives a simple outcome for this overall loss ratio.
