14   Viscous loss

14.1   Introduction

Viscous loss is the "leadscrew" part of the power loss.

  This "leadscrew" acts in a flow accelerated by the pro­peller's own thrust, but the screw itself does not know it. It just threads itself forward relative to the surrounding air, even if that air is slipping backward.

  In a physical leadscrew, the friction force is proportional to the normal force on the contact surface. The ratio is called the friction coefficient, usually indicated by μ ( the Greek letter mu ). This ratio is also the tangent of the angle at which a friction block would begin to slide down a slope.

  In a propeller, the friction coefficient or angle is usually called ε. It is the inverse of the so-called glide ratio Cl ⁄ Cd of the blade sections, the slope along which a glider would descend in free flight. At the design stage, it makes sense to dimension the prop blades to work at their best Cl ⁄ Cd, so at that point we can consider the "friction angle" of the blade sections as an optimal constant.

  In off-design conditions this will no longer be true. At off-design lift values, the section drag is closer to a con­stant Cd at varying Cl. There are also secondary effects of the Reynolds and Mach numbers. These are best solved by numerical methods.

Figure 14.1 :   Horizontal force pushing a block up a slope.

  Figure 14.1 shows a horizontal force pushing a block up a slope. On a slope of 1:10, the horizontal force F travels ten times the distance of the vertical ( weight ) force W. From the power balance ( where power equals force times displacement ), the force F which keeps the block from sliding needs to be only one tenth of the force W, in the ideal case with no friction.

  Force F will have to move the friction force over 10 times the distance of the vertical displacement. This costs 10 times more friction power than if F could have lifted the force W directly. The frictional loss relative to weight is ( approximately ) inversely proportional to the slope. The analysis in the next section will confirm this.

  Most mechanical leadscrews more or less have a single slope angle, for a single diameter almost at the outer radius. Their thread does not cut very deep. In propellers, the "thread" goes right to the core. This causes considerable pitch angle variation with radius. Therefore we will subdivide the prop disk radially into rings, and analyze each radius separately.

  The friction force is proportional to the load. Unlike the momentum loss, the frictional efficiency does not de­pend on the loading. It only depends on the average radius where the load applies, and on the local friction coefficient.

14.2   Leadscrew efficiency

We now look at the lift and drag forces on a blade element at radius r. Figure 14.2 shows the force diagram. We assume light loading for the moment, so the actual pitch angle φ′ is approximately the same as the nominal pitch angle φ = atan ( V / Ω r ) for the free stream conditions.

  In more precise calculations, we can substitute the actual φ′ for φ.

Figure 14.2 :   Thrust and torque on a blade element.

The forward velocity of the airplane is V, and the propeller rate of rotation is Ω ( in rad/s ). The local lift force L is ortho­gon­al to the local velocity W. The drag D is that part of the aerodynamic force which is orthogonal to L, so it is parallel again to the local velocity W. The tilted result­ant of L and D is often called R, but to avoid confusion with the prop radius we will call it L′ here.

  The drag D is an order of magnitude smaller than L, and therefore we can interpret L′ as a slightly rotated version of L. The backward angle of tilt ε ( in radians ) follows from the ratio between D and L. In wing sections this is called the "glide angle". It is the inverse of the section "glide ratio" L ⁄ D :

tan ε ≡ D L = C d C l (14.1)

We can also project the resultant L′ along alternative axes, decomposing it into a thrust force dT, and a tan­gent­ial force dS at right angles to it. The "d" prefix signifies "contribution of a single ring" :

d T = L  ′  . cos ( φ + ε ) (14.2)
d S = L  ′  . sin ( φ + ε ) (14.3)

The net power exerted by the ring on the airplane is :

d T . V = L  ′  . cos ( φ + ε )  .  V (14.4)

Acting at a radius of r, the tangential force dS exerts a torque around the propeller shaft of dS . r :

d Q = d S . r (14.5)

The power needed to turn the propeller is the shaft torque Q, multiplied by the rotation rate Ω ( in rad/s ).
With (14.3) for dS, the shaft power is :

d Q . Ω = L ′  . sin ( φ + ε ) .  Ω   r (14.6)

The viscous efficiency of the blade element is net output power over shaft input power :

d  η v = d  T . V d  Q . Ω = cos ( φ + ε ) sin ( φ + ε ) . V Ω   r (14.7)

With tan φ = V ⁄ Ω r, we have :

d  η v = tan φ tan ( φ + ε ) (14.8)

  This formula goes back to Froude. It is the standard efficiency equation for mechanical leadscrews with friction angle ε. For zero friction, i.e. for ε = 0, the propeller is like an ideal leadscrew. It will convert shaft torque into forward thrust with perfect efficiency.

  For pitch angles φ of ( 90°- ε ) and higher, the efficiency becomes zero. The leadscrew seizes, or "locks up". In propellers, this condition occurs close to the hub. We will ignore it, and analyze the efficiency only for moderate angles of φ and small angles of ε.

14.3   The viscous loss ratio

Equating (14.8) to the efficiency definition (3.10), we have :

d  η v = 1 1 + d P ^ v d T ^ = tan φ tan ( φ + ε ) (14.9)

Inverting this gives :

1 + d  P ^ v d  T ^ = tan ( φ + ε ) tan φ (14.10)

which can be rewritten as :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ = tan ( φ + ε ) − tan φ tan φ (14.11)

Figure 14.3 shows this loss ratio as a function of the local pitch angle φ.

 Textbooks on mechanical drive components give a full analysis of (14.8) which we will not repeat here.

In the region beyond φ = 90° - ε the leadscrew is "self locking". The figure shows this region as a shaded vertical bar, scaled here for ε = 0.02.

Figure 14.3 :   The local leadscrew loss ratio as a function of the local pitch angle φ.

  The viscous efficiency is optimal for a pitch angle of ( 45° - ½ ε ). It is left-right symmetrical around this vertical line. At the optimum point, the loss ratio is nearly 2 ε. If ϵ is small, the optimum angle is close to 45°.

  Most of the thrust in a propeller is delivered on the outboard part of the blade, at relatively flat pitch angles. Such low pitch angles are favorable with respect to rotational momentum loss, but they are the least favorable with respect to blade section profile drag. From a viscous point of view, all thrust would have to be concentrated in a short paddle near the radius where the pitch angle is 45°. Simple wind turbines with sheet metal blades of poor lift‑to‑drag ratio ε = Cd ⁄ Cl in fact use exactly this arrangement, in a ring of many square blades.

14.4   A near- perfect approximation

Expression (14.10) for the frictional loss ratio can be greatly simplified with almost no loss of precision, by noting that the numerator is a finite difference between two tangents. Since the friction angle ε is usually small, we can approximate this difference by ε times the derivative of the tangent. That derivative is 1 ⁄ cos2 φ :

tan ( φ + ε ) − tan φ ≅ ε . 1 cos 2 φ (14.12)

This changes (14.11) into :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ = ε . 1 cos 2 φ . 1 tan φ (14.13)

Using tan φ = 1 ⁄ x from (2.4), we have a simple yet almost exact expression which is easiest remembered in mixed coordinates φ and x :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ = ε . x cos 2 φ (14.14)

Eliminating φ completely by (2.8), this is the same as :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ = ε  . (  x + 1 x  ) (14.15)

Figure 14.4 shows the exact loss ratio (14.10) as a solid dark blue line, and the near perfect approximation (14.11) as a dashed line. This is the same figure as figure 14.3, only this time with x = 1 ⁄ tan φ on the horizontal axis instead of φ. This swaps left and right in the picture. The highest pitch angles are now near the left vertical axis, and the smallest pitch angles are far to the right. The "middle of the range" where φ = 45° is at x = 1. This skews the whole bath­tub figure of 14.3 to the left.

  The viscous loss factor reaches a minimum of 2 . ε  near x = 1, the point where φ equals 45°.

  For all but the smallest values of x, i.e. for all pitch angles except φ ≅ 90°, the approximation (14.14) is very nearly perfect.

Some of the literature, like Glauert, will set cos2 φ ≅ 1. This turns (14.14) into :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ ≅ ε . x (14.16)

Figure 14.4 shows this further simplification as a straight line.

Figure 14.4 :   The local viscous loss ratio as a function of x.

This last equation comes closest to an interpretation of the viscous loss as the "projected", or "geared" friction coefficient ε. In the vector diagram of figure 14.2, for small angles φ a relatively small force dS can drive a larger thrust force dT, by a "gearing" factor of x = 1 ⁄ tan φ. In a similar way, a small friction force along the surface can become a larger "drag" force in the thrust direction.

14.5   Vector derivation of the same approximation

The nearly exact loss ratio of (14.14) and (14.15) can also be loosely derived directly from the force diagram. We split the viscous drag vector D into two orthogonal components, which we shall call ΔdT and ΔdS.

  In the force diagram in figure 4.2 we can see with some effort that :

Δ d   S ≅ D . cos   φ
Δ d   T ≅ −   D . sin   φ (14.17)

We also have

D ≅ ε . L (14.18)

and

dT ≅ L . cos   φ (14.19)

which yields :

D ≅ ε . d T cos   φ (14.20)

Using this in (14.17) and (14.18) we have :

Δ d   T ≅ − ε . d   T cos φ . sin φ = ε . d  T  . tan φ (14.21)

and :

Δ d   S ≅ ε . d   T cos φ . cos φ = ε . d  T (14.22)

With some hand waving, we can say that if we multiply these two drag forces each by their local parallel veloci­ties into powers, we will obtain two terms independently contributing to the local power loss due to profile drag.

  The axial drag component Δ dT directly opposes the flying velocity V. It causes a reduction of the net output power T . V. The tangential drag component Δ dS directly opposes the circumferential blade element velocity Ω r. It causes an increase in the shaft power needed to turn the propeller. Both detract from the thrust ⁄ power ratio, and we will call both a power loss. The total local viscous power loss is :

d  P v ≅ Δ d  T . V + Δ d  S .  Ω   r (14.23)

Substituting (14.17) and (14.18) and dividing by dT . V, the local loss ratio is :

d  P v d  T . V ≅ d  T . ε . ( tan   φ . V + Ω   r ) d  T . V = ε . ( tan   φ + Ω   r V ) (14.24)

With (2.2) and (2.4) we have tan φ = 1 ⁄ x and Ω r ⁄ V = x. Making the ratio non-dimensional with (3.9) gives :

d  P ^ v d  T ^ = d  P v d  T . V = ε . (   1 x + x ) = ε . x cos 2 φ (14.25)

This recovers (14.15), and therefore (14.14). This derivation shows that the linearization (14.16) amounts to looking only at the viscous drag effect on the torque, and neglecting its effect on the thrust.

14.6   Neglecting a small momentum term

The power calculation of (14.23) misses a subtle point. The viscous drag component ΔdT of (14.17) detracts from the propeller's thrust, and to regain the original thrust we need to increase the non-viscous thrust by that small amount. This will cause a small additional momentum loss for the same net thrust. However, since this is a second order effect in the thrust, we can neglect this term for propellers of light loading.

  We can get a quick estimate of this term as follows. The extra momentum drag follows from the extra ring thrust needed ε ⁄ x . T ^ in (14.21), multiplied by the marginal loss T ^ ⁄ κφ of (11.8) for the whole propeller (how do we reconcile the ring to the propeller ?).

The extra momentum term is in ( T ^ )2, so it is a second order effect in T ^ . Since it is a second order effect, we can neglect this term for propellers of light loading.
